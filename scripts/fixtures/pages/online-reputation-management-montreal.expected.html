Why it works here

Two audiences reading
the same star rating.

The mechanics of reputation management are the same everywhere, and they are written up in full on the online reputation management page. What changes in Montreal is who is reading, and in which language.

A francophone customer scrolling your reviews is not reading the average. They are reading the French ones, and if the three most recent are unanswered while the English ones all have polished replies, that gap says something the star rating does not.

This is the most common finding when we audit a Montreal business. Not a bad reputation. A reputation that was managed attentively in one language and left to look after itself in the other.

What we handle here

Six things that are
different in this market.

These are the parts of a reputation engagement that get rewritten for a Montreal business rather than copied from a national playbook.

None of them are exotic. They are the details that get skipped by an agency running the same process from another province, and they are the ones a local customer notices immediately.

01

Replies written in French

A review left in French gets an answer written in French, not run through a translator. The difference is visible in a sentence, and a stiff reply to a complaint reads as a second insult rather than an apology.

02

Asking in the right language

Review requests go out in whichever language the customer dealt with you in. A francophone client asked in English will usually leave nothing at all. The review profile then misrepresents half the customer base, and nothing on it explains why.

03

Profiles per location

A business on the island, one on the South Shore, and one in Laval need three accurate profiles, not one shared listing with an address that sends people across a bridge they did not need to cross.

04

Names, accents and spelling

A business name written with accents in one listing and without them in the next is a small problem that repeats across every directory. It splits reviews, confuses matching, and is tedious enough that nobody gets around to fixing it.

05

The local directories

Quebec still has directory sites that rank for a business name here and nowhere else. Half of them hold information nobody has updated in years, and they sit on page one because nothing has been published to take the position.

06

Monitoring in both languages

Mentions get tracked in French and English, including the accented and unaccented spellings of your name. Watch only one spelling and a discussion can run for a month before anyone inside the business hears about it.

The language question

Answer them in the
language they wrote in.

The rule is simple and it is applied without exceptions. French review, French reply. English review, English reply. It costs nothing, and francophone customers read it as the clearest available signal of whether you take them seriously.

Businesses break this rule for practical reasons rather than careless ones. Whoever handles reviews is comfortable in one language, so the French ones wait, then get a short reply, then eventually get none. The pattern is legible to anyone scrolling.

Machine translation is not the shortcut it looks like. Quebec French has its own vocabulary and register, and a reply assembled from a translation tool lands somewhere between formal and foreign. Under Quebec's language rules the French version of customer-facing communication also has to be at least as prominent as the English, which is worth knowing before a bilingual template gets designed.

FAQ

Straight answers
on Montreal.

Do you answer reviews in French?+
Yes, and they are written in French rather than translated from an English reply. A review left in French gets a French answer, an English one gets an English answer, and a reviewer who switches between the two gets whichever language they used most. A Quebec reader spots a machine-translated reply immediately, and that undoes most of the point of replying.
How much do Google reviews matter for a Montreal business?+
For anything a customer finds on a map, they are close to the whole decision. Someone comparing three options nearby has a star rating, a review count, and the two or three newest comments to work from. For businesses where the sale involves a conversation, reviews matter less at the start and quite a lot at the point where someone checks you before signing.
We have several locations around Montreal. Does that change things?+
It changes the setup more than the work. Each location needs its own accurate profile with its own reviews, hours, and address, and the reviews should not be pooled. A South Shore location carrying the ratings of a downtown one tells a customer nothing useful, and it hides the location that actually has a problem.
Can you remove a negative review?+
Only where there are grounds. A review that breaks Google's policy, or one from someone who was never a customer, can be reported and is often removed. A genuine bad experience cannot be, and it should not be. Those get a public answer, and the rating gets moved by asking more customers for reviews rather than by deleting the ones you dislike.
